3  Nonparametric Structural Equations

In the previous section we introduced potential outcomes, addressed some general questions about how to interpret them, and got a preview of how assumptions or prior knoweldge can be used to overcome the fundamental problem of causal inference. We did this mainly in the context of a hypothetical study comparing two drugs. In this section we’ll formalize a few of the ideas we touched on in the last section by placing them into the broader framework of structural causal models.

Along the way, we’ll show how to generalize potential outcomes beyond the single-timepoint binary treatment setting (like the drug study we discussed). Even if you are only interested in that setting, understanding how potential outcomes are used more broadly to define causal effects for continuous treatments, multiple treatments, mediation, and treatments that vary over time can be very helpful to shore up your understanding of how potential outcomes work at a fundamental level. We’ll also discuss censored data problems and domain transfer as alternative applications of similar ideas.

Let’s start with the following definition:

Causal Model

Definition 3.1 A causal or full-data \({\mathcal{P}}^*\) is any statistical model (in the sense of Definition 1.1) where some of the random variables in each of the constituent distributions \({\mathbb{P}}^*\) are considered to be intervention variables and some are considered potential outcomes. Let’s start by formalizing what we mean by “single-timepoint treatment”. This is how we’ll generalize the structure of the new vs. old drug example discussed above. The framework we’ll use to do that is that of a statistical model (Definition 1.1).

Observational study with a single-timepoint, binary treatment

Definition 3.2 What we’ll call the standard observational study model is the set of all distributions over variables \((Y,A,X)\) where:

  • \(Y \in \mathcal Y \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) (the outcome)
  • \(A \in \{0,1\}\) (the treatment, exposure, or intervention)
  • \(X \in \mathcal X \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d\) (the covariates)

This model imposes no prior knowledge of the true data-generating distribution other than the domains of each of the variables. More often than not, this is all we know confidently when data are collected observationally. By observational study, we mean a study where the analyst did not have control over the way the treatments were allocated for the observations in the dataset.

Other authors use different conventions for the variables in an observational study. \(Y\) is fairly standard for outcomes, but in econometrics \(W\) is more often used for the exposure, whereas in the targeted maximum likelihood literature, \(W\) is used for the covariates. I like \(Y\) and \(X\) as outcome and covariates because it mirrors the usual notation for machine learning and regression where \(X\) would be the predictors of \(Y\). I like \(A\) for treatment because, as a letter near the beginning of the alphabet rather than at the end, it more clearly stands out from the variables that are not hypothetically intervened on. You can also imagine \(A\) standing for \(A\text{ction}\).

There are many examples of the standard observational study setup. For instance, if the drug data we described above in Table 2.1 were not from a randomized study but instead were extracted from electronic health records, then we would have an observational study.

Example 3.1 (Effect of high school on college enrollment) As another example, consider a study where school records are compared to see if children who go to a particular private high school have higher rates of immediate college enrollment than those in another school. Unless the analyst had control over who enrolled in what school, this would be an observational study where \(A\) represents which school each student enrolled in and \(Y\) represents whether or not they went to college after graduation (also as a binary indicator). In data such as these we may also have access to covariates \(X\) such as the identified sex of the student, their middle school grades, some markers of parental socioeconomic status, etc.

While we might suspect certain co-occurence relationships between the variables (e.g. studets with Catholic parents may be over-represented at a Catholic school), it’s unlikely that we know enough to quantify those suspiscions as modeling assumptions.


Example 3.2 (Effect of fertilizer on fall crop yield) Suppose we’re interested in the effect of fertilizer on crop yield. If we were to survey a number of farmers and ask about their crop yields (\(Y\)) and whether or not they used a specific fertilizer earlier in the year (\(A\)), this would constitute an observational study because we did not directly control who was using what fertilizer; the farmers each decided for themselves. In this example, we may also include survey questions about covariates \(X\) like where the farms are located, previous history of fertilizer use, the type of crops grown, etc.

History and Notes

In the Western tradition, causality has been a topic of philosophical debate at least as far back as Aristotle (Aristotle 1984b, 1984a). Counterfactual theories of causality have more immediate roots in Hume (1748) and Mills (1843) – Holland (1986) gives a concise review of these texts.
